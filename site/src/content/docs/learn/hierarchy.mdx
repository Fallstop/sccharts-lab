A washing machine is more than on and off. Once you press start, it runs a programme: it fills the
drum, washes, and drains. The phases of the programme belong together, and some things have to work
whichever phase the machine is in. The stop button is one of them.

Every chart so far has been a single, flat state machine. A **superstate** expresses this grouping:
it is a state with states inside it. From the outside it is one state (in the chart below it is
called `Programme`); inside, it is a small state machine of its own. This chapter shows how a chart
enters and leaves a superstate, how declarations inside one behave, and how a superstate finishes.

## A state with states inside

Here is the washing machine's controller. It waits in `Idle` until `start`, then runs the programme.
The inputs `full` and `timeUp` come from the machine's sensors: the drum is full, the wash time is
over.

<Sim example="learn/hierarchy/washer" caption="Step to tick 2 and look at the diagram: `Programme` and `Filling` are both active." />

`Programme` is declared like any other state, followed by a block in braces. Inside the braces you
write states and transitions exactly as you would at the top of a chart, with one of them marked
`initial`. This is what KIELER does with it, tick by tick:

| Tick | `start` | `full` | `timeUp` | State after | `motor` after |
| ---: | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | false | false | false | Idle | false |
| 2 | true | false (held) | false (held) | Programme (Filling) | false |
| 3 | false | false (held) | false (held) | Programme (Filling) | false |
| 4 | false (held) | true | false (held) | Programme (Washing) | true |
| 5 | false (held) | false | false (held) | Programme (Washing) | true |
| 6 | false (held) | false (held) | true | Programme (Draining) | false |
| 7 | false (held) | false (held) | false | Programme (Draining) | false |

Three things to notice:

- **Entering a superstate enters its initial state in the same tick.** Tick 2 takes a single
  transition, `Idle` to `Programme`, and ends with two active states: `Programme` and, inside it,
  `Filling`. A superstate is never active on its own; one of its inner states is always active as
  well. When superstates are nested, entering the outer one enters every level, all the way down,
  in the same tick.
- **Inside, the usual rules hold.** `Filling` was entered in tick 2, so its delayed transition can
  fire from tick 3 on. An immediate transition out of `Filling` could have fired in tick 2 already
  (see [Delayed and immediate transitions](/learn/immediate/)).
- **Moving between inner states does not leave the superstate.** The machine changes phase in
  ticks 4 and 6, but `Programme` stays active the whole time.

KIELER draws a superstate as a large rounded box with its name at the top and its inner states
inside. Declarations of the superstate, if it has any, are listed under its name, the way the chart
lists its inputs and outputs. In SCCharts Lab the diagram also follows the editor's cursor: put the
cursor inside `Programme`'s braces, and the diagram selects `Programme` and expands the regions
around it, which in a large chart is the quickest way to see where you are.

## Leaving from anywhere

The machine needs a stop button that works in every phase. You could give each of `Filling`,
`Washing` and `Draining` its own `if stop go to Idle`. A superstate lets you write it once:

<Sim example="learn/hierarchy/stop" caption="In tick 4, `stop` leaves `Programme` while it is in `Washing`. In tick 5 the machine starts again at `Filling`." />

The transition `if stop do motor = false go to Idle` is written after `Programme`'s closing brace,
so it belongs to `Programme` itself, not to any state inside it. It can fire whichever inner state is
active, and when it fires, the inner state is left along with `Programme`.

Two details matter in practice:

- **The superstate does not remember where it was.** When `Programme` is entered again in tick 5,
  it starts at its initial state, `Filling`. A machine that should resume where it stopped needs a
  history transition, which comes much later, in
  [History and deferred transitions](/advanced/history/).
- **Leaving undoes nothing.** That is why the stop transition switches the motor off. Without
  `do motor = false`, a stop during `Washing` would leave `Programme` with `motor` still true, and it
  would stay true in `Idle` and after the next start: a variable keeps its value until something
  assigns it.

A transition written on a superstate, after its closing brace, is called an **abort**: it can cut
short whatever the inside is doing. The `go to` you have used all along makes a *weak* abort: if a
transition inside is enabled in the same tick as `stop`, the inside takes its step first, and only
then is `Programme` left. Its counterpart, the *strong* abort `abort to`, leaves before anything
inside moves. [Aborts](/learn/preemption/) is about the difference.

<Aside type="caution" title="Transitions stay on their own level">
A transition connects two states on the same level: both inside the same superstate, or both at the
top of the chart. You cannot jump into the middle of a superstate. `if start go to Washing`, written
on `Idle`, is rejected with `Couldn't resolve reference to State 'Washing'.`: seen from `Idle`'s level
there is no state called `Washing`. A superstate is always entered at its initial state. In the same
way, `Washing` cannot go straight to `Idle`. The ways out of a superstate are its own transitions,
like `stop`, and a final state with `join to`, which comes below.
</Aside>

## Declarations inside a superstate

A superstate can declare its own variables and signals. They go at the start of its block, before
its first state, in the same way as the chart declares its inputs and outputs at the top.

### The nearest declaration wins

A declaration inside a superstate is a **local** of that superstate. The transitions inside it can
use the name; the rest of the chart cannot. A transition outside the superstate that used one of
its locals would be rejected with `Couldn't resolve reference to ValuedObject '…'.`, naming the
variable.

Every superstate is a **scope**, and scopes nest. If the same name is declared in several of them, a
use of the name means the nearest declaration, looking outwards from where it is used. The inner
declaration hides, or *shadows*, the outer one:

<Sim example="learn/hierarchy/shadow" show={['press', 'count', 'Outer.count', 'seen']} caption="Watch the two `count` lanes: the output `count` stays 1, because the transition updates `Outer.count`, `Outer`'s own, from 10 to 11. `seen` copies it." />

KIELER does not warn about shadowing. The chart works, but whoever reads it (you, next week) can
easily mistake one `count` for the other. Give locals names of their own.

Two more facts about scope, both of which surprise people:

- `input` and `output` only mean something at the top of a chart. Written inside a superstate,
  `output int z` declares an ordinary local: it is not part of the chart's interface, and nothing
  outside the chart can see it.
- A superstate's own outgoing transitions, the ones written after its closing brace, can read the
  superstate's locals too. KIELER's validator has a check that was meant to forbid this, but the
  check never fires, so such charts compile. Don't build on it: if the way out of a superstate
  depends on a variable, declare the variable one level further out.

### Fresh on every entry

A rep counter for the gym shows the repetitions of the current set. A press on the button counts a
rep; `rest` ends the set; the next press starts a new set.

<Sim example="learn/hierarchy/reps" caption="In tick 9 the first rep of the second set shows 1, not 3: `reps` started again from 0 when `Set` was entered in tick 7." />

`reps` is declared in `Set`, so it is initialised every time `Set` is entered: in tick 1, and again
in tick 7. (The press of tick 7 only starts the set: `Counting` is entered in that tick, and its
transition is delayed.) That is the rule for every local: **a variable declared in a superstate gets its initial
value on every entry of the superstate**, or, if it has none, its type's default (`0` for `int`,
`false` for `bool`). Think of a superstate as a function and its locals as the function's local
variables: every call starts from scratch.

For the reps of one set, that is what you want. A value that has to survive leaving and re-entering,
such as the total over all sets, belongs one level further out, at the top of the chart. You will
build it in the second exercise below.

## Finishing: final states and `join to`

So far the programme never ends: the machine drains for ever. Let the drain lead to a final state,
and let `Programme` react when its inside has finished:

<Sim example="learn/hierarchy/finish" caption="In tick 5 three things happen at once: `Draining` moves to `Done`, `Programme` terminates, and the join takes the machine back to `Idle` with a beep." />

`Done` is a final state, drawn with a double outline (see
[Delayed and immediate transitions](/learn/immediate/)). At the top level of a chart a final state
ends the chart; inside a superstate it ends the superstate's inner state machine. When the inside of
a superstate reaches a final state, the superstate **terminates**.

Termination on its own does not move anything: a terminated superstate stays active until one of its
transitions leaves it. The transition written `join to` is a **termination transition**: it fires
when its superstate has terminated. KIELER draws it with a green triangle where it starts, as on the
transition from `Programme` back to `Idle` in the diagram above. It is drawn dashed because it is
immediate, which is the next point.

In detail:

- **The join fires in the tick the superstate terminates.** In tick 5 the inner transition to `Done`
  and the join both fire, and the beep belongs to that same tick.
- **The join's effects run after everything the inside did in that tick.** If the inside writes a
  variable on its way to the final state, the join's effect already sees the new value.
- **A join without a trigger is immediate.** It is checked in every tick in which its superstate is
  active, including the tick in which the superstate was entered.

The diagram numbers `Programme`'s two outgoing transitions: `1: stop` and `2: / beep`. They are
priorities, as for any state with several outgoing transitions (see
[Triggers, effects and priority](/learn/transitions/)). What happens when a stop and the end of the
programme fall into the same tick is a question for [Aborts](/learn/preemption/).

### In and out in one tick

Because a trigger-less join is immediate, a superstate whose inside finishes at once is left again
in the tick it was entered. Predict what this chart does when `start` arrives, run it, then open the explanation:

<Predict example="learn/hierarchy/quick" ask={[{ tick: 2, value: 'steps' }, { tick: 2, state: 'Finished' }]} caption="`Begin`'s transition is `immediate`, and the join has no trigger." />

<details>
<summary>Explanation</summary>

In tick 2, `Idle` to `Work` fires and sets `steps` to 1. Entering `Work` enters `Begin`, whose
immediate transition fires in the same tick: `steps` is 2 and `Work` reaches its final state, `Done`.
`Work` has terminated, and its join is immediate, so it fires too: `steps` is 3 and the chart is in
`Finished`. Three transitions in one tick, and `Work` was active for no time at all.

</details>

### A join with a trigger waits a tick

A termination transition can have a trigger: `if ready join to After` fires once its superstate has
terminated *and* `ready` holds. Like every transition with a trigger, it is then delayed: it is not
checked in the tick its superstate was entered, even if the superstate terminates in that tick and
the trigger is true. Compare the two versions:

<Compare a="learn/hierarchy/join-plain" b="learn/hierarchy/join-guarded" scenario="learn/hierarchy/join-compare" caption="`ready` is true throughout and `start` comes in tick 2: `PlainJoin` reaches `After` in tick 2, `GuardedJoin` one tick later." />

The diagrams show the difference as well: `PlainJoin`'s join is drawn dashed, because it is
immediate, and `GuardedJoin`'s solid, because it is delayed. The four ways to write a join, and
whether each is checked in the tick its superstate is entered:

| Termination transition | Checked in the entry tick? |
| --- | --- |
| `join to After` | yes: no trigger, so immediate |
| `if ready join to After` | no: a trigger makes it delayed |
| `immediate if ready join to After` | yes |
| `delayed join to After` | no |

After the entry tick, a delayed join is checked in every tick for as long as its superstate stays
terminated, so it fires in the first tick in which its trigger holds.

<Aside type="caution" title="A join with nothing to wait for">
`join to` needs an inside that can finish. On a simple state KIELER rejects it:

```text wrap
A state with a termination transition must have inner behaviour.
```

On a superstate without a final state it only warns (a *region* is the state machine inside a
superstate; the next chapter has superstates with several):

```text wrap
Every region should have a final state whenever its parent state has a termination transition.
```

The join can then never fire. KIELER skips even this warning when the superstate has
[actions](/learn/actions/) of its own, and on a few charts of this shape its compiler fails
outright. The fix in every case: give the inside a final state it can reach, or leave with an
ordinary transition instead of `join to`.
</Aside>

<Aside type="caution" title="A signal and an action stop termination">
In KIELER, a superstate that declares a [signal](/learn/signals/) and also has an entry, during or exit
action ([chapter 10](/learn/actions/)) never terminates, even when its inside reaches a final state:
its `join to` never fires. Declare the signal one level further out. The chart itself is the extreme
case: KIELER's simulator gives every chart an action of its own, so a chart that declares any signal
never terminates ([Signals and termination](/learn/signals/#signals-and-termination)).
[My `join to` never fires](/reference/troubleshooting/#my-join-to-never-fires) shows the stuck and
the fixed chart side by side.
</Aside>

## Superstates inside superstates

A superstate can contain superstates, which can contain superstates, to any depth. The rules are the
same at every level:

- Entering a superstate enters its initial state, and if that is a superstate, its initial state,
  and so on, all in one tick.
- A transition of a superstate leaves it whichever state is active inside it, however deep.
- Termination travels outwards. When an inner superstate terminates and its join leads to a final
  state of the outer superstate, the outer one terminates in that same tick, and its own join can
  fire as well.

A coffee machine shows the last rule. `Brew` sits inside `Make`, and both finish with a join:

<Sim example="learn/hierarchy/coffee" caption="Tick 5: the cup is full, and `Brew`, then `Make`, terminate in the same tick. Watch `cups` and `ready`." />

In tick 5 three transitions fire, from the inside out: `Pouring` to the final state `Poured`, which
ends `Brew`; `Brew`'s join, which counts the cup and enters `Served`, a final state of `Make`; and,
since `Make` has now terminated as well, `Make`'s join, which emits `ready` and returns to `Idle`. Both
joins have no trigger, so both are immediate and fire in the tick their superstate terminates.

The first exercise builds a rinse cycle this way.

## Check your understanding

<Quiz
  question="A superstate `S` has the termination transition `if c join to T`. Its inside reaches a final state in the tick `S` is entered, and `c` is true in that tick and in the next. When does the join fire?"
  options={['In the tick `S` is entered', 'In the tick after that', 'Never: the final state was reached before the join was checked']}
  answer={1}
>
A join with a trigger is delayed, like every transition with a trigger, so it is not checked in the
tick `S` is entered. In the next tick `S` is still terminated and `c` is still true, so the join
fires. `GuardedJoin` above does exactly this. Without the trigger, `join to T` would have fired in
the entry tick.
</Quiz>

<Quiz
  question="A chart declares `output int n = 0`. Its superstate `S` declares `int n = 5`, and a transition inside `S` does `n++`. What is the output `n` after that transition?"
  options={['0', '1', '6']}
  answer={0}
>
Inside `S`, the name `n` means the nearest declaration, `S`'s own local. That local goes from 5 to 6.
The output `n` is shadowed inside `S` and nothing assigns it, so it is still 0.
</Quiz>

<Quiz
  question="`Set` declares `int reps` without an initial value. The chart leaves `Set` while `reps` is 4, and later enters `Set` again. What is `reps` right after the new entry?"
  options={['4', '0', 'It has no defined value']}
  answer={1}
>
Variables do keep their values, but a local of a superstate is initialised again on every entry of
the superstate: to its initial value if it has one, otherwise to its type's default, `0` for an
`int`. `RepCounter` above shows the same re-initialisation, there to its initial value, 0: the first
rep of the second set, in tick 9, shows 1.
</Quiz>

<KeyIdeas>
- A superstate is a state with states inside. Entering it enters its initial state in the same
  tick, all the way down.
- A superstate's own transitions, written after its closing brace, leave it whichever inner state
  is active. The next entry starts again at the initial state.
- Declarations inside a superstate are local to it. The nearest declaration wins, and locals are
  initialised again on every entry.
- When the inside reaches a final state the superstate terminates, and `join to` can fire in that
  same tick. A join without a trigger is immediate; `if c join to` waits at least one tick.
</KeyIdeas>
